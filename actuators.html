Actuators

How a person asks an object to do something. And how the object answers.

01How to read this volume

Every actuator here is treated as a species. Each entry has the same fields, so two species can be compared line by line: how to identify it, when to use it and when not, how to proportion it, how it is made, how it feels and sounds, which species it is confused with, where it lives (its habitat: real products), and where it appears in my own work.

Numbers marked [verify] are starting points from common practice, not laws. Test them on a printed sample before trusting them. That is what the sample is for.

02Four questionsAn identification key

Before choosing an actuator, answer four questions. They decide most of the design.

02.1What does the finger do? Press, flip, slide or turn. This sets the gesture, and the gesture sets the form. 02.2Does it stay where you leave it? A latching actuator shows its state. A momentary one forgets it the moment you let go, so something else (a light, a screen, a sound) has to say what happened. 02.3Steps or continuous? Steps are for choices (off, low, high). Continuous is for amounts (volume, tuning). Detents turn an amount into choices. 02.4Can you use it without looking? Shape, travel, clicks and end stops are how a hand reads an object in the dark.

Try the key. Answer the questions and it names the species.

11Lab: feel and soundUse headphones

Four actuators, synthesised. None of them is real, which is the point: the difference between them is mostly in what happens at the end of the gesture.

Momentary push

Two sounds: one on the way down, a softer one on the way up. Nothing stays.

Toggle

One snap. The lever shows the state from across the room.

Rotary

Drag up or down. With detents, each step ticks; without, the knob is silent and the eye has to read the scale.

Slider

Silent by design. Position is the information, and you can read it without touching it.
